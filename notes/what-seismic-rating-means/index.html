What a Seismic Rating Actually Promises You

LeDeuxions · notes from twenty years on site

New apartment advertising puts the seismic rating in large type. People read it and conclude: this building will not fall down in an earthquake. That is not what it says. What it says is more limited than that, and more useful than that.

What the rating is actually for

The standard is written around one objective: give the people inside enough time to get out. It is not a guarantee the structure survives. It is a guarantee it does not fail instantly — that the frame holds together through the shaking long enough for you to move.

What happens to the building afterwards is a separate question. It may be damaged past the point of being usable. It may need to come down. The rating does not say "will not collapse." It says "will not collapse yet."

The tiers, and what they mean

Most countries use the same logic under different names — Korea has its own grades, the US assigns Risk Categories, and the structure of the idea is common to all of them:

Essential facilities. Buildings that have to keep working after the ground stops moving. Hospitals, fire stations, emergency operations, power. These are designed to a materially higher standard because the day after matters as much as the day of.

Standard occupancy. Life safety and collapse prevention. Apartments, offices, schools. This is where nearly everything is.

Low occupancy. Reduced demand, for structures where few people are at risk.

Here is the part that gets lost. Engineers design to the standard tier by default. That is the baseline. So when a development advertises that it meets it, that is not an achievement being announced — it is compliance being announced.

Why the advertising bothers me

I will say this plainly: seeing "seismic rated" in large letters on a hoarding is a little strange. It is not a feature. It is the floor. It reads like a car advertised as having seatbelts.

What would actually be worth advertising is either an essential-facility standard, or a specific number — by how much the design exceeds the requirement, at what drift limit, with what redundancy. When none of that is offered, the rating in the advertising is a marketing line, not evidence of performance.

Use what it gives you

None of this is meant to frighten anyone. It is the opposite. The structure is buying you seconds, and you can only spend them if you know they exist.

When it starts: get low, under a table, cover your head. When the shaking stops, leave immediately. Never the lift. Get to open ground away from the facade — falling cladding is a serious hazard and it lands outward.

That sequence takes time. The time is exactly what the engineering provides.

What it is and is not

A seismic rating is not a boast and it is not absolute safety. It is one specific promise: that people get out alive. A building keeping that promise is already worth something. If you want more than that, you want a building designed to the essential-facility standard — and you should ask for the number, not the label.

In construction there is almost never an absolute, and that is most true in front of an earthquake. But the seconds it buys you are not vague. They are calculated.
